Introduction

This past Friday (October 2020), on my way dropping Jia-En off at school, some unpleasant things from the past came to mind. I prayed to God, “Lord, please take away all the hatred in my heart, and keep it filled with passion for You; please work the miracle of the five loaves and two fish upon my limited love, so that I may look forward to and experience Your wonders every day.” I then made a few edits and posted it online. What I wanted to say is that everyone’s life may go through unpleasant times, but what matters most is how you choose your future—and that is our theme today: forgiveness.

Do Not Judge for Lack of Forgiveness

Luke 6:37 says, do not judge. The word “judge” here, krinō (κρίνω), means choosing to criticize, find fault with, and condemn others—to pass sentence on them.

The Bible tells us not to criticize or judge others, yet we often judge and criticize people because we differ with them, or because our inner defense mechanisms kick in after we have been hurt. But whatever the reason we judge, we must remember what the Lord Jesus tells us through Scripture: we must not condemn others.

Some brothers and sisters point to 1 Corinthians 6:3, which asks, “Do you not know that we will judge angels? How much more the things of this life!” The word “judge” there is the same Greek word as “judge” in Luke. It is indeed the same word, but to understand Scripture we must look not only at the meaning of the original word but also at its context.

Bible · 1 Corinthians 6:1–8

If any of you has a dispute with another, do you dare to take it to court before the unrighteous, instead of taking it to the saints? Do you not know that the saints will judge the world? And if you are to judge the world, are you not competent to judge trivial cases? Do you not know that we will judge angels? How much more the things of this life! Therefore, if you have disputes about such matters, do you ask for a ruling from those whose way of life is scorned in the church? I say this to shame you. Is it possible that there is nobody among you wise enough to judge a dispute between believers? But instead, one brother takes another to court—and this in front of unbelievers! The very fact that you have lawsuits among you means you have been completely defeated already. Why not rather be wronged? Why not rather be cheated? Instead, you yourselves cheat and do wrong, and you do this to your brothers and sisters.

From the context we see that Paul is saying you may “krinō,” but here it does not mean our subjective criticism and judging. When believers have lawsuits among themselves, they should be brought to the church; the church can appoint wise brothers and sisters as a team to help discern. It is something like a judging court within the church, but not an organ of power. Rather, out of love, they judge matters between brother and brother in the name of the Lord. And we are able to judge matters within the church in only one situation: when a dispute between brothers has been entrusted by God into the church’s hands. The church must also be on guard, so that the spirit of medieval inquisition does not grow up among us. The church judges out of love, not out of a claim to subjective power and control.

Will the church’s judging repeat the mistakes of church history? I believe it will not, when it judges on the basis of mutual love. When lawsuits arise in the church, can the church judge rightly? On one hand, I believe that when the church judges in the Lord’s name, God gives wisdom and revelation. On the other hand, Paul even encourages brothers and sisters to be willing to suffer loss for the Lord’s sake, rather than oppressing or defrauding others—rather than one brother suing another, and doing so before unbelievers.

Read in its biblical context, you see that Paul is not saying we may judge people according to our own subjective ideas. Whether we burn with gossipy curiosity, or feel ourselves deeply wounded and hurt, we must not judge people privately; rather, we are publicly entrusted by the church to judge disputes between brothers within the church.

You hypocrite, first take the plank out of your eye, and then you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother’s eye.”

Returning to Luke 6, note that before the “you” in “do not judge… do not condemn…” there is a particle, kai (καί, pronounced “kai”), that has not been translated; it implies: even if it is a fact, we still must not judge. Two things stand out. First, our knowledge is limited; we may offer subjective guesses, and what we judge may be wrong. Second, even if what we judge is correct, God does not want us to judge—unless, as Scripture says, brothers and sisters have wronged one another and the matter has been entrusted to the church for judgment.

The Butterfly Effect of Unforgiveness

Scripture tells us “do not judge or condemn others, so that you will not be judged or condemned.” This does not mean not being judged by people, but not being judged and condemned by God. When we are hurt by others, choosing forgiveness means that before God we find release and freedom from that hurt. When we do not forgive, on one hand our soul stays in prison, clinging to the grievance; on the other hand, an unforgiving and bitter heart also breeds spiritual poison that destroys our bodies.

Scripture also says, “Give, and it will be given to you. A good measure, pressed down, shaken together and running over, will be poured into your lap. For with the measure you use, it will be measured to you again.” You may have heard this verse preached when the church asks us to give offerings, but this verse is actually about forgiveness. Because we do not forgive, the consequences of our unforgiveness are measured back to us, pressed down and shaken together—this is why bitterness and hatred consume us.

It can be said that unforgiveness leaves our bodies ravaged by past wounds. Forgiveness is choosing to let God repay justice; for us it means letting go, no longer hating, and setting ourselves free. In the body and soul pain caused by bitterness, choosing forgiveness is choosing healing. Before forgiveness we were blind; after forgiveness, the blind receive their sight.

Bible · Luke 17:1–4

Jesus said to his disciples: “Things that cause people to stumble are bound to come, but woe to anyone through whom they come. It would be better for them to be thrown into the sea with a millstone tied around their neck than to cause one of these little ones to stumble. So watch yourselves. If your brother or sister sins against you, rebuke them; and if they repent, forgive them. Even if they sin against you seven times in a day and seven times come back to you saying ‘I repent,’ you must forgive them.”

How Many Times Must We Forgive?

How many times must we forgive? Jesus teaches that if someone sins against you, rebuke them; if they repent, forgive them. If they sin against you seven times in a day and seven times turn back, saying, “I repent,” you must forgive them. What Jesus speaks of here is timely repentance and timely forgiveness; do not let the sun go down while you are still angry.

Bible · Matthew 18:21–35

Then Peter came to Jesus and asked, “Lord, how many times shall I forgive my brother or sister who sins against me? Up to seven times?” Jesus answered, “I tell you, not seven times, but seventy-seven times. Therefore, the kingdom of heaven is like a king who wanted to settle accounts with his servants. As he began the settlement, a man who owed him ten thousand bags of gold was brought to him. Since he was not able to pay, the master ordered that he and his wife and his children and all that he had be sold to repay the debt. At this the servant fell on his knees before him. ‘Be patient with me,’ he begged, ‘and I will pay back everything.’ The servant’s master took pity on him, canceled the debt and let him go. But when that servant went out, he found one of his fellow servants who owed him a hundred silver coins. He grabbed him and began to choke him. ‘Pay back what you owe me!’ he demanded. His fellow servant fell to his knees and begged him, ‘Be patient with me, and I will pay you back.’ But he refused. Instead, he went off and had the man thrown into prison until he could pay the debt. When the other servants saw what had happened, they were greatly distressed and went and told their master everything that had happened. Then the master called the servant in. ‘You wicked servant,’ he said, ‘I canceled all that debt of yours because you begged me to. Shouldn’t you have had mercy on your fellow servant just as I had on you?’ In anger his master handed him over to the jailers to be tortured, until he should pay back all he owed. This is how my heavenly Father will treat each of you unless you forgive your brother or sister from your heart.”

Imitate Jesus Without Ceasing

Jesus taught that when a brother sins against you and repents, you must forgive him seven times a day. So Peter asked Jesus, “Shall I forgive my brother seven times?” If we put ourselves in Peter’s place, it is easy to see that Peter felt he was doing quite well—and indeed he was. Peter was doing wonderfully, better than most of us. But Jesus does not want us to rely on our own effort to keep the law; he wants us to consider what he has accomplished for us. His cross—was that not forgiveness toward us, again and again? When we consider his boundless forgiveness toward us in this life, only then can we forgive others seventy-seven times! Imitating Jesus Christ without ceasing.

How Great the King’s Pardon

Jesus tells of a man who owed the king of the kingdom of heaven ten thousand talents and could not repay. The king ordered that this debtor, his wife and children, and all he had be sold to repay. But because the debtor begged the king for mercy, the king, filled with compassion, canceled his debt. Yet when that man met a friend who owed him a hundred denarii, he would not forgive his friend, but had him thrown into prison. When the king learned of this, he was very angry and said to him, “You wicked servant! I canceled all that debt of yours because you begged me. Shouldn’t you have had mercy on your fellow servant just as I had on you?” And he handed him over to the jailers until he should pay back all he owed.

That debtor is us. On the cross Jesus sacrificed himself for our sins, for all our incompleteness, so that through trusting in him we might have new life. Yet we often hate those who sin against us. Are we not exactly the people who have forgotten that we ourselves were once forgiven by God? Compared with the wrongs and hurts others have done to us, how great is the sacrifice of Jesus!

Unforgiveness Hands Us to the Jailers

Bible · Matthew 18:34–35

In anger his master handed him over to the jailers to be tortured, until he should pay back all he owed. This is how my heavenly Father will treat each of you unless you forgive your brother or sister from your heart.

Verse 34 tells us that unforgiveness hands us over to the jailers—and these jailers are the devil. Our unforgiveness hands us into the devil’s hands to be tormented, so that body and soul are constantly racked. It is not that the devil has some great power or authority; rather, our unforgiveness gives the devil authority to harm us. Only when we choose forgiveness and rely on the power of the cross of Jesus are we freed from the dominion of the devil’s power.

Choosing Forgiveness Sets You Free

After forgiveness, the cords that bind and torment us no longer strangle us tightly, and we can overcome through Jesus. When we choose to let the cross of Jesus heal us, our ropes fall completely away!
